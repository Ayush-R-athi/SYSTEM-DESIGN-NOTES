What is a Video? (First Principles)

Before understanding video streaming, you need to understand what a video actually is at a technical level.

A video = a sequence of images (frames) played in rapid succession.
Your brain perceives them as smooth motion — the same trick that makes a flipbook "animate."
🎬 How Frames Become a Video
  1 image = 1 frame (a snapshot of a moment)

  Play many frames rapidly one after another:

  [Frame 1] [Frame 2] [Frame 3] ... [Frame 60]
     →          →         →              →
     └──────────────────────────────────┘
               one second of video!

  Frame Rate (FPS) = how many images per second:
  ┌──────────┬──────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  1 FPS   │ Choppy, looks like a slideshow           │
  │ 20 FPS   │ Acceptable, slightly stuttery            │
  │ 30 FPS   │ Standard video (YouTube default)         │
  │ 60 FPS   │ Smooth (gaming, sports)                  │
  │ 120 FPS  │ Super smooth (high-end displays)         │
  └──────────┴──────────────────────────────────────────┘

  Higher FPS = smoother motion = LARGER file size

Why is Video File Size So Large?

📦 Back-of-Envelope: Video File Size
  A 4K video at 60 FPS, 10 minutes long:

  1 uncompressed 4K frame ≈ ~25 MB (3840×2160 pixels, 24 bits each)
  60 frames/second × 25 MB = 1,500 MB per second
  10 minutes × 60 seconds × 1,500 MB = 900,000 MB ≈ 900 GB (raw!)

  After compression (H.264 codec): ≈ 4–6 GB
  Still massive for streaming!
🎞️
Video formats (MP4, MKV, MOV) are like ZIP files for frames. They use compression algorithms (called codecs like H.264, H.265/HEVC, VP9, AV1) to reduce the file size dramatically while keeping the video watchable.

The Problem — Why Video Delivery is Hard

The core engineering challenge of video streaming is this: how do you deliver a 4–5 GB file to a user in real-time across wildly different devices and network conditions?

📱

Mobile Phone

Small screen, 4G/WiFi, limited battery, needs 360p–1080p

💻

Laptop

Medium screen, good WiFi, needs 720p–1080p

📺

4K TV / LED

Big screen, wired/WiFi, needs 4K

⌚

Smartwatch

Tiny screen — does it even need HD?

🚗

Car/Tablet

Variable network, medium screen

🚌

On the Move

Spotty network, dropping signal, needs graceful degradation

⚠️ The hard constraint: The same 4K video file on a server must somehow play perfectly on a smartwatch with 2G internet AND on a 4K LED TV with 1Gbps fibre — simultaneously. A one-size-fits-all approach fails everyone.

Era 1 — Progressive Download (The Old Way)

In the early 2000s, there was no concept of streaming. The approach was simple — and painful.

Early 2000s
Progressive Download User clicks play → server sends the ENTIRE video file → user must wait for all of it to download before watching. A 100 MB video = wait for 100 MB.
📥 Progressive Download Flow
  User clicks "Play" on a 4 GB video
          │
          ▼
  Server starts sending the ENTIRE file
          │
  ████████████████████░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░
  ← downloaded (1.2 GB)   (still coming...)
          │
  User waits... and waits... (buffering ⏳)
          │
  ████████████████████████████████████
  ← fully downloaded (4 GB)
          │
          ▼
  NOW you can finally watch the video ✅
  
  Problems:
  ❌ Long wait before playback starts
  ❌ Downloads data you may never watch (skip the video at 2 mins)
  ❌ Wastes bandwidth on abandoned views
  ❌ Fails on slow connections — 4 GB × 10 Mbps = 53 minutes!
This is where "buffering" came from! The old spinning circle while "loading" was literally waiting for the download to reach the point you want to watch. Classic early YouTube / dial-up internet experience.
4

Era 2 — Real-Time Streaming Protocols (RTMP / RTSP)

Mid 2000s
RTMP & RTSP Protocols Adobe built RTMP (Real-Time Messaging Protocol). RealNetworks built RTSP (Real-Time Streaming Protocol). These enabled sending video in chunks — no need to download the full file first.

📡 RTMP — Real-Time Messaging Protocol

  • Built by Adobe (the Flash company)
  • Low latency streaming
  • Live streaming support
  • Send video in chunks (not whole file)
  • Client plays as chunks arrive
  • Efficient bandwidth use

📡 RTSP — Real-Time Streaming Protocol

  • Built by RealNetworks
  • Similar goals to RTMP
  • Used in cameras, IP streams
  • Still used in security cameras today
  • Also specialised streaming protocol
✅ What RTMP/RTSP solved: You no longer needed to wait for the full download. Video starts playing immediately as chunks arrive. If you stop watching at 2 minutes, the rest isn't downloaded — huge bandwidth savings!
❌ What RTMP/RTSP did NOT solve — The Quality Problem:
These protocols sent the video in one fixed quality — whatever the original was. If the source was 4K and your phone had only 10 Mbps internet, each chunk was still large and took too long. You'd get buffering mid-video. And a smartwatch got a full 4K chunk it didn't need!
5

Era 3 — Adaptive Bitrate Streaming (The Modern Way)

Today
Adaptive Bitrate Streaming (ABR) The video is pre-encoded into multiple quality levels. The client (your phone/TV) picks the best quality level it can handle in real-time and switches dynamically as conditions change.
The core idea of ABR: Instead of one video at one quality, store the same video at multiple qualities (240p, 360p, 480p, 720p, 1080p, 4K). Let the client decide what's best for it — and let it switch mid-video if network conditions change.
🎯 Adaptive Bitrate — The Magic Moment
  You're watching on your phone (good WiFi, 100 Mbps):
  → Client says: "I can handle 1080p!" → streams 1080p ✅

  You get on the metro, signal drops to 5 Mbps:
  → Client detects: "Slow network!" → switches to 480p seamlessly
  → Video keeps playing (maybe slightly blurry) — no buffering ✅

  You exit the metro, back to good WiFi:
  → Client detects: "Fast network again!" → switches to 1080p ✅

  The video never stopped. Quality changed — but playback continued.

❌ Without ABR

  • One fixed quality for everyone
  • Slow network → video stops (buffering)
  • Smartwatch gets same 4K file as 4K TV
  • No graceful degradation
  • Quality doesn't match device capability

✅ With ABR

  • Multiple quality options, client picks best
  • Slow network → lower quality, keeps playing
  • Each device gets what it can handle
  • Dynamic quality adjustment in real-time
  • Used by YouTube, Netflix, TikTok, Instagram
🚰
Water pipe analogy: Without ABR = one fixed pipe size. Narrow pipe (bad network)? Water (video) can't flow fast enough. With ABR = adjustable pipe. If the pipe is narrow, use a thinner stream of water (lower quality). If wide, use the full flow (4K). Never dry (buffering)!

The Video Processing Pipeline

For ABR to work, a LOT of processing happens before a video goes live. This is why YouTube says "your video is processing" for 10–30 minutes after you upload.

⚙️ What Happens When You Upload a Video to YouTube
  You upload: Original video (4K, 4 GB MP4)
          │
          ▼
  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │           PROCESSING PIPELINE                       │
  │                                                     │
  │  Original 4K video → split into time segments       │
  │                                                     │
  │  For each segment, encode at multiple qualities:    │
  │  ├── Encode to 240p  (very small, very slow nets)   │
  │  ├── Encode to 360p  (mobile on 3G)                 │
  │  ├── Encode to 480p  (mobile on 4G)                 │
  │  ├── Encode to 720p  (standard HD)                  │
  │  ├── Encode to 1080p (Full HD)                      │
  │  └── Encode to 4K    (for big screens, fast nets)   │
  │                                                     │
  │  Generate manifest/index file (M3U8 or MPD)         │
  └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
          │
          ▼
  All segments + index file stored on CDN/Object Storage
          │
          ▼
  Video is LIVE and streamable 🎉
1
Receive raw upload Creator uploads original video (any format — MP4, MKV, MOV). This is stored as-is temporarily.
2
Transcode to multiple bitrates The video is encoded into 240p, 360p, 480p, 720p, 1080p, 4K versions. Each quality is a separate encoded version. This is GPU-intensive and expensive.
3
Split into segments (chunks) Each quality version is split into small ~2–10 second chunks called segments. A 10-minute video at 720p might have ~60 segments of 10 seconds each.
4
Generate index / manifest file A special file (M3U8 for HLS, MPD for DASH) is created that lists all available qualities and the location of every segment. The client reads this first.
5
Upload everything to CDN / Object Storage All segments and the manifest file are pushed to a CDN (like CloudFront). Video is now globally available with low latency.
⚠️ Storage multiplication: A 4 GB 4K video, after encoding at 6 quality levels, might become 7–10 GB total (lower qualities are smaller, but you have many of them). Multiply this by millions of videos and you need petabytes of storage — exactly why we did that calculation in Video 5's notes!

Segments + The Manifest / Index File

This is the core data structure that makes ABR streaming work. Let's understand segments and the manifest file in detail.

What are Segments?

🧩 Video Segments — 10-Minute Video at 720p
  Original 720p video: 10 minutes long
          │
  Split into 10-second segments:
          │
  ┌─────┐ ┌─────┐ ┌─────┐ ┌─────┐ ┌─────┐ ... ┌──────┐
  │ S1  │ │ S2  │ │ S3  │ │ S4  │ │ S5  │     │ S60  │
  │0-10s│ │10-20│ │20-30│ │30-40│ │40-50│     │590-60│
  └─────┘ └─────┘ └─────┘ └─────┘ └─────┘     └──────┘
  
  Same video at 480p → another 60 segments
  Same video at 1080p → another 60 segments
  Same video at 4K    → another 60 segments
  
  Total segments for this one video: ~240 files!

What is the Manifest / Index File?

📋 M3U8 Manifest File — What it Contains
  File: index.m3u8  (the "table of contents" of the video)

  Contents (simplified):
  ────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  #EXT-X-STREAM-INF:BANDWIDTH=500000,RESOLUTION=640x480
  /videos/myvideo/480p/playlist.m3u8     ← 480p segments here

  #EXT-X-STREAM-INF:BANDWIDTH=1000000,RESOLUTION=1280x720
  /videos/myvideo/720p/playlist.m3u8     ← 720p segments here

  #EXT-X-STREAM-INF:BANDWIDTH=3000000,RESOLUTION=1920x1080
  /videos/myvideo/1080p/playlist.m3u8    ← 1080p segments here

  #EXT-X-STREAM-INF:BANDWIDTH=8000000,RESOLUTION=3840x2160
  /videos/myvideo/4k/playlist.m3u8       ← 4K segments here
  ────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  And the 480p playlist.m3u8 contains:
  ────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  #EXTINF:10.0,
  segment_001.ts    ← segment 1 (0–10 seconds)
  #EXTINF:10.0,
  segment_002.ts    ← segment 2 (10–20 seconds)
  ...
  ────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The manifest file is like your notebook's table of contents. It doesn't contain video data itself — just a map of where every segment lives. The client reads it first, then decides which quality playlist to follow, then downloads segments one by one.

HLS vs MPEG-DASH

There are two major formats for Adaptive Bitrate Streaming. They do the same thing — just in slightly different ways.

📱 HLS — HTTP Live Streaming

  • Created by Apple
  • Manifest file: .m3u8
  • Segment files: .ts (MPEG-TS)
  • Native support on iOS, Safari, Apple TV
  • Most widely used on the web
  • Works over standard HTTP (no special server)
  • Used by YouTube, Twitch, TikTok

🌐 MPEG-DASH — Dynamic Adaptive Streaming over HTTP

  • Open standard (ISO/IEC)
  • Manifest file: .mpd
  • Segment files: .mp4 fragments
  • Codec-agnostic (more flexible)
  • Supported by Chrome, Firefox, Edge
  • Also works over standard HTTP
  • Used by Netflix, Amazon Prime
✅ Bottom line for interviews: Both HLS and MPEG-DASH achieve Adaptive Bitrate Streaming. HLS is more widespread (especially for live streams). DASH is more flexible. Most modern platforms support both. The concepts — segments, manifest, client-side quality switching — are identical for both.

How the Client Adapts — The Algorithm

The "adaptive" in ABR doesn't happen by magic. The client (your video player app) runs a continuous algorithm to decide which quality segment to download next.

🔄 Client ABR Algorithm (Continuous Loop)
  Step 1: Download manifest file (index.m3u8)
          "Ok, I can have 240p, 360p, 480p, 720p, 1080p, 4K"

  Step 2: Start with LOWEST quality (safe default)
          "I'll start at 240p — we don't know the connection yet"

  Step 3: Measure current network speed
          (download speed = how fast the last segment arrived)

  Step 4: Decision loop for EVERY next segment:
          ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────┐
          │ if current_speed > bitrate_of_quality_above: │
          │   upgrade quality (e.g. 480p → 720p)         │
          │ elif current_speed < bitrate_of_this_quality: │
          │   downgrade quality (e.g. 720p → 480p)       │
          │ else:                                        │
          │   stay at current quality                    │
          └──────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  Step 5: Download next segment at chosen quality
  Step 6: Go to Step 3 (repeat for every segment)
The exact algorithm varies by platform. Netflix uses "Buffer-based" ABR. YouTube uses a hybrid. Some use screen size as a factor (no point downloading 4K if your screen is 360p). The player decides — not the server. The server just stores all the segment options.

Real Example — Client Switching in Action

📊 Quality Switching Over Time
  Time  Network Speed  Quality Selected
  0s    Unknown        240p  ← start low, safe
  10s   80 Mbps        720p  ← network is great, upgrade!
  20s   80 Mbps        1080p ← still great, upgrade more!
  30s   70 Mbps        1080p ← still fine, stay
  40s   15 Mbps        720p  ← dropped, downgrade
  50s   5 Mbps         480p  ← dropped more, downgrade again
  60s   3 Mbps         360p  ← barely working, downgrade
  70s   60 Mbps        720p  ← back on WiFi, upgrade!
  80s   80 Mbps        1080p ← excellent again, max quality
  
  User experience: Video NEVER stopped. Quality changed — no buffering.

Full Summary & Architecture Overview

The Evolution of Video Streaming

Era 1
Progressive Download Download entire video first. Then play. Huge wait. Bandwidth wasted. Called "buffering." Still used for small files.
Era 2
RTMP / RTSP Streaming Stream in chunks. Play as they arrive. No full-download wait. But one fixed quality — no adaptation to network/device.
Era 3 (Now)
Adaptive Bitrate Streaming (HLS / MPEG-DASH) Pre-encode video at multiple qualities. Client picks best quality per segment. Dynamically adapts as network/device conditions change. No buffering. No wasted bandwidth.

Key Terms to Remember

🎬

Frame / FPS

A video = images played rapidly. FPS = frames per second. Higher FPS = smoother but bigger file.

📥

Progressive Download

Full file downloads before playback. Old, slow, wasteful. The original "buffering."

📡

RTMP / RTSP

Specialized streaming protocols. Stream in chunks. Live support. But fixed quality.

🎯

ABR Streaming

Multiple quality levels. Client picks best in real-time. Adapts to network & device.

🧩

Segments

Small 2–10 second chunks of video at each quality level. Client downloads one at a time.

📋

Manifest File

The "index" — lists all available qualities and where every segment file is located. M3U8 (HLS) or MPD (DASH).

📱

HLS

HTTP Live Streaming by Apple. Uses .m3u8 manifest. Most widely used, especially on iOS.

🌐

MPEG-DASH

Open standard ABR format. Uses .mpd manifest. More codec-flexible. Used by Netflix.

⚙️

Transcoding Pipeline

Converts uploaded video into multiple quality segments before going live. Why YouTube takes 20–30 mins to process.

📌 Engineering reality: Building your own ABR pipeline is extremely complex and expensive — requires GPU clusters for encoding, massive object storage, global CDN, and constant maintenance. That's why most startups use managed services like ImageKit, AWS Elemental MediaConvert, Cloudflare Stream, or Mux for video processing and delivery.
